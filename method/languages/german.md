# Notes for German sources

Prepared before the first German vector translation. Revise from practice.

## Rights first
- Germany, Austria, the UK and the rest of the EU: life plus 70 years, counted to the end of the calendar year. In 2026 an author who died in 1955 or earlier is in the public domain.
- Most of the Frankfurt School proper is in copyright: Adorno (d. 1969), Horkheimer (d. 1973), Marcuse (d. 1979), Kracauer (d. 1966), Bloch (d. 1977), Löwenthal (d. 1993), Sohn-Rethel (d. 1990), Kirchheimer (d. 1965). Translating and publishing these needs the rights holder. Check every date against an authority file (GND, VIAF) before relying on it.
- In the public domain, among others: Walter Benjamin (d. 1940), Henryk Grossmann (d. 1950), Franz Neumann (d. 1954), Georg Simmel (d. 1918), Emil Lask (d. 1915). Check each.
- The Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung is a good hunting ground, but rights go essay by essay with each author's death date.
- United States: works published before 1931 are in the public domain in 2026; later works depend on renewal and other factors. Record both statuses.
- A modern critical edition's apparatus (notes, variants, editorial corrections) has its own rights. Translate from the first or a public-domain edition where possible, and record which.

## Images and OCR
- Sources: Internet Archive, HathiTrust (full view varies by country), Deutsches Textarchiv, DigiZeitschriften, university and state library digital collections (many with IIIF).
- Fraktur OCR is now serviceable (Tesseract Fraktur models, OCR4all, Calamari), unlike pre-war Japanese. Test it on a sample page (rule 1). If it holds, it can serve as a draft source checked against the images, with known faults to watch: long s (ſ) read as f, ligatures (ch, ck, tz, ſt), hyphenated line ends, and the umlaut forms of older printing. Antiqua is easier.
- Single pages or spreads: work out the mapping as for any source.

## Conventions to decide before the pilot
- **The author's own notes.** German academic prose carries its own footnotes, unlike Kuki. The author's notes and the translator's need separate marking: for example, the author's numbered as in the original, the translator's lettered or set in the author's sequence with the prefix "Tr.".
- **Brackets for insertions.** German authors and editors use square brackets themselves. Options: keep 〔 〕 from the Kuki convention, which cannot be confused with the author's; or ⟨ ⟩; or square brackets with an editorial note. Record the choice and the reason in the Translator's Note.
- **Emphasis.** Letter-spaced type (Sperrdruck) and italics as italics; record where the author uses both for different purposes.
- **Quotations of German classics.** For quotations of Hegel, Marx, Kant, Goethe and others, decide between translating from the author's text and using standard English editions with references (for example the Marx-Engels Collected Works, the Cambridge Kant), noting any modification. The Kuki rule (translate from the author's text) was made for an author quoting through Japanese; a German author quoting German originals may call for the other choice.
- **Titles and dates.** Whether titles of works are given in German with English in a note, and how references to German editions are normalised in the readable version.

## Syntax and register
- Verb-final clauses, long periodic sentences, nominalisation and compounds. A close literal version is easier than from Japanese; the readable version faces the real register decision.
- Breaking a long periodic sentence changes the argument's shape. For authors whose difficulty is part of the argument, the translator should set the register on a sample page before any agent drafts (rule 6). Over-smoothing was the main fault class on the first run (rule 7).
- Watch for memorised wording from published translations in the readable version. It is a fidelity problem and a copyright one; the read-through brief should ask checkers to flag passages that match a published translation.
